1986–1998 · Cardy, holography, and BTZ entropy

Counting states you have not met

How symmetry gets the black-hole entropy right before we know the full microscopic story.

Imagine being able to estimate the population of a city without meeting its residents. You would need unusually powerful constraints. In two-dimensional conformal field theory, modular invariance supplies something close to that power: it relates a thermal regime dominated by low-energy states to another that samples high-energy ones.

Cardy’s formula turns this relation into an asymptotic count.[1] It is not a census of individual states. It is a statement about how rapidly their number grows under suitable assumptions.

For a black hole, that can be exactly the question we need.

The arithmetic is almost suspiciously good

Let \(h\) and \(\bar h\) denote the plane conformal weights, and let \(c_L=c_R=c\). Write the dimensionless left- and right-moving cylinder energies, including the vacuum Casimir shift, as \(E_L=h-c/24\) and \(E_R=\bar h-c/24\). In the regime where the Cardy estimate applies, the leading entropy takes the form

$$S\simeq2\pi\sqrt{\frac{cE_L}{6}}+2\pi\sqrt{\frac{cE_R}{6}}.$$

For BTZ, \(E_L=(\ell M+J)/2\) and \(E_R=(\ell M-J)/2\). Combine these with the Brown–Henneaux value \(c=3\ell/(2G)\), and the leading result becomes the horizon circumference divided by \(4G\). Strominger’s 1997/1998 argument made this agreement a central piece of the story.[2]

No arbitrary numerical adjustment is needed to rescue the coefficient. Geometry, boundary symmetry, and CFT asymptotics line up.

What the success tells us

The agreement says that a suitable boundary CFT description has the right asymptotic capacity to account for the black-hole entropy. That is a substantial achievement. It connects a gravitational thermodynamic quantity to a microscopic counting framework.

But universal formulas are universal precisely because many different systems share them. A correct leading count need not single out one spectrum, much less one operator algebra. Two cities can have the same population and very different street plans.

There is also a regime question. At fixed central charge, Cardy is a high-energy asymptotic statement. Extending its usefulness into a large-central-charge gravitational regime requires control of the light spectrum; the work of Hartman, Keller, and Stoica makes the role of sparsity precise.[4] It would be careless to apply the expression indiscriminately to every black hole arbitrarily close to a threshold.

Holography makes the question sharper

Maldacena’s AdS/CFT proposal supplied a much broader setting in which boundary quantum theories describe bulk gravity.[3] String-theoretic examples can come with explicit microscopic constructions and additional fields. They make holography concrete, but they do not automatically settle the separate problem of pure three-dimensional Einstein gravity.

“Find some AdS₃ quantum gravity with a CFT dual” and “identify the exact dual of a chosen pure-gravity theory” are different assignments. The second asks for a particularly austere bulk content while retaining consistency at every energy and in every observable.

We can now see why the subject remains lively after the entropy calculation. The leading number is right. The complete object that produces the number remains a more demanding target.

One way forward is to turn austerity into a rule: keep the states forced by boundary gravity, postpone other states as long as consistency permits, and see whether a CFT can survive. That is the temptation behind the extremal proposal. It is a much sharper question than “what might the boundary be?”—and a sharper question has more ways to fail.
